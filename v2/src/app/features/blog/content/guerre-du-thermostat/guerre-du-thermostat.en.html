🔥 Heater on full blast and window wide open: the roommate fight the Cloud replays without knowing it! 🥶

Hey network! 👋 It's cold in Lille, and this scene must be playing out right now in thousands of apartments. 🧣 The first roommate is always cold and turns the heater to maximum. The second one gets hot fast and opens the window wide. Each of them reacts perfectly to what they feel… and yet the heater runs all day, cold air pours in all day, the bill climbs and nobody is comfortable! 😅 And I find EXACTLY this fight inside the Cloud! 🤓☁️

In my test store (TeaStore), the Recommender service (the one that suggests products to visitors) has a switch: normal mode or low power mode. With AdaptiFlow, it can follow several adaptation rules at the same time, and each rule has its own hand on that switch. Three roommates, each with their own thermometer: 🌡️🌡️🌡️

  • 🩹 The healing rule watches the database: "it's back online, normal mode!"
  • 📈 The optimization rule watches the CPU: "it's above 75%, low power mode!"
  • 🛡️ The protection rule watches the traffic: "attack in progress, low power mode and connections cut!"

Taken one by one, all three rules are right. But picture an evening where the database comes back RIGHT in the middle of a traffic peak: one rule switches normal mode back on, the other switches it off again, and the service spends its whole evening turning its own heater on and off. 🔥🥶🔥

🤔 Who referees?

Nobody, and that is on purpose! Each rule decides alone, with no conductor, and that is what keeps the services simple and independent… as long as the rules don't fight! 🙃 In the MAPE-K loop (monitor, analyze, plan, execute), we are here at the moment of acting, when two hands land on the same switch.

I wrote it in black and white in my paper: adaptations can conflict with each other, and the next step it announces is to give the rules a way to agree before touching the switch, with priorities written down in advance. In short, nobody should ever open the window without glancing at the heater! 🤝

💡 The moral of the story

If you keep just one thing: two good decisions, each made in its own corner, can add up to one bad decision. 💡
