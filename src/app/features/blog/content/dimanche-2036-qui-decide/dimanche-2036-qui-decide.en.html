🔮 It's 2036 and a server goes down in the middle of the night… so who decides what to do? 🌙

A little Sunday-night game: let's jump ten years ahead! 🕰️ Servers will have changed, outages… not so much (a database that stops answering at 3 am never goes out of style 😅). So to decide what happens next, three candidates step forward:

  • 😴 Candidate 1, the human: woken up by their phone, they look for their glasses, then for the server password (any resemblance to the author of this post is purely coincidental 🙈).
  • 📜 Candidate 2, the rule: written in advance by a human, and the server applies it on its own ("if the database stops answering, serve what you have in memory and tell the other services").
  • 🤖 Candidate 3, the AI agent: it looks at the situation and improvises an answer no human ever wrote down.

Today, AdaptiFlow runs my microservices with candidate 2. When my test store gets attacked or overloaded, its services switch to protection mode or low power mode on their own, and none of those decisions were made in the middle of the night: every one of them was written, in Java, weeks earlier, on a quiet Tuesday afternoon! 😎

But I deliberately gave the observation and the action of those services the same standard shape, so that one day I can plug candidate 3 in place of the rules and compare the two: which one reacts better, and at what cost in resources? ⚖️🤖 That's one of the next steps my 2025 paper announces.

And since it's Sunday, here's my unfiltered opinion: I don't believe AI will replace engineers in 2036 (no more than in 2026 😏). I do believe it will change what we ask them to write: less "what to do when it breaks", more "what the machine is allowed to do". ✍️

Because autonomic computing doesn't remove the human decision: it moves it from the panic of 3 am to the calm of a Tuesday at 3 pm. 💡
